Robotics / Embedded vision

Vision
tracking robot.

A robot that could find a person, follow them, and keep its distance.

I built a four-wheel robot that paired camera-based human tracking with independent distance sensing. A Raspberry Pi 5 handled perception; an STM32 handled the motors.

The build

Getting the whole robot to work together.

The goal was to detect a person, keep them centered in the camera view, and maintain a set distance as they moved. I used the Pi Camera 3 for visual input and a time-of-flight sensor for distance feedback, then connected that information to the drivetrain through the STM32.

The demonstration also had a mechanical response: after the person stopped for two seconds, a flywheel mechanism launched foam balls. That made the project about more than following a bounding box—it also involved coordinating perception, movement, and a timed behavior.

System architecture

Two processors. Different jobs.

I separated the camera and sensor processing from the physical motor-control layer. The Pi interpreted what the robot was seeing; the STM32 operated the drivetrain and mechanism.

Computer vision

The neural network was MobileNet-SSD.

My presentation lists Detector.py → MobileNet-SSD. This was the person-detection part of the system: camera frames went through the detector, and the detected person's bounding box supplied position information for tracking.

MobileNet is a lightweight convolutional neural-network architecture designed for mobile and embedded vision. SSD stands for Single Shot MultiBox Detector; it predicts object categories and bounding boxes in one network pass. Together, they provide a compact object-detection approach.

Feedback & behavior

Seeing a person was only the start.

Visual feedback

Center the person

The horizontal offset of the detected person's bounding box from the center of the camera view supplied a centering error. As the person moved across the frame, that error supplied the information needed to correct the robot's heading.

Distance feedback

Maintain separation

The ToF sensor added a direct distance measurement. It let the robot maintain a following distance using range feedback, instead of treating the size of a person's image as a distance measurement.

Motor response

PID control

I used PID control to adjust motor response as the tracking error changed. The STM32 operated the motors while the Pi handled the camera and sensor processing. Getting turning corrections to behave well was part of joining those two layers.

2 s

A separate timed condition

For the demo, I set a two-second stationary-person condition before the foam-ball flywheel response. This was a behavior condition layered onto the tracking and distance-control work.

What took work

The integration problems were the project.

01

Power for the Pi

The Pi 5 needed more current than expected during integration. I used external power for the Pi, making power distribution a separate problem from controlling the motors.

02

UART pin configuration

Only certain STM32 pins could be configured for the required UART connection. The communication link had to fit the board's available peripheral configuration.

03

Detection within a compute budget

Tracking needed to be accurate and robust without becoming too computationally demanding. Running the vision work on the Pi made that tradeoff part of the system design.

04

Range alignment and stale readings

The ToF sensor was not always aligned with the person seen by the camera. The presentation also records stale readings on poorly reflective surfaces—finding a person in an image did not automatically mean getting a useful range measurement.
